Desert Heat Headaches: Why Vegas Summers Hurt and What Helps

By the Wongu Health Center team · October 9, 2026 · 5 min read

It's 112°F at 6 p.m., the steering wheel is too hot to touch, and that dull ache behind your eyes has been building since lunch. If summer in Las Vegas seems to bring more headaches, you're not imagining it. Between the heat, the bone-dry air, and bouncing from blazing parking lots into ice-cold casinos and offices, desert living asks a lot of your body. Here's why it happens, and what can help.

Yes, Heat Can Trigger Headaches

Researchers studying more than 7,000 emergency room visits for headache found that higher air temperatures in the day before a visit were linked to more severe headaches. For about every 9°F rise, the risk went up by roughly 7.5%.[1] In a city where summer highs sit well above 100°F for weeks at a time, that adds up.

Several things tend to stack up here:

  • Dehydration. Dry desert air pulls moisture from you faster than you notice. Not drinking enough water is a well-recognized headache trigger, and for many people it eases within an hour of drinking water.[2]
  • Hot-to-cold swings. Walking from a 110°F parking garage into a heavily air-conditioned building and back again, over and over, can leave you feeling off.
  • Bright sun and glare off windshields, pools, and pavement, a common trigger for people prone to migraine.
  • Sleep and schedule changes. Hot nights, late shifts, and summer travel can all throw sleep off, which is another common trigger.
  • Tight neck and shoulders. Stress and long days often settle into your neck, setting the stage for tension headaches.

Simple Ways to Head Off a Heat Headache

  • Sip all day, not all at once. Keep a water bottle with you. If you're sweating a lot or working outside, adding electrolytes can help.
  • Eat water-rich foods like watermelon, cucumbers, oranges, and soups.
  • Go easy on alcohol and extra caffeine on the hottest days, since both can leave you more dehydrated.
  • Wear sunglasses and a hat when you're out in the midday sun.
  • Plan outdoor time for early morning or after sunset when you can.
  • Bring a light layer for over-air-conditioned spaces, so the hot-cold swings are gentler on you.
  • Notice your patterns. A simple note on your phone of when headaches hit can reveal your personal triggers.

How Acupuncture May Help

Headaches are one of the areas where acupuncture has the strongest research behind it. A Cochrane review, one of the most rigorous kinds of research summary, found that acupuncture can reduce how often migraines happen, and that it worked at least as well as some common preventive medicines, with fewer side effects.[3]

In Chinese medicine, summer heat is seen as something that can drain the body's fluids and energy, and your practitioner will look at the whole picture: your sleep, stress, digestion, and where your headaches show up. Treatment may include acupuncture, releasing tight neck and shoulder muscles, and, when appropriate, a cooling herbal tea selected after checking your medications. Learn more on our headaches and migraines page.

When a Headache Is More Than a Headache

In extreme heat, a headache can be an early sign of heat illness, so please take care of yourself and the people around you:

  • Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, cool and clammy skin, dizziness, nausea, cramps, or weakness. Move somewhere cool, loosen your clothes, cool down with wet cloths, and sip water. Get medical help if you're vomiting, getting worse, or not better within an hour.[4]
  • Heat stroke: a very high body temperature, hot red skin, a fast pulse, confusion, or passing out. Call 911 right away. Move the person somewhere cooler and cool them down while you wait.[4]

Also seek care right away for a sudden, severe “worst headache of my life,” or a headache with a stiff neck, fever, confusion, weakness, numbness, or trouble speaking or seeing.
