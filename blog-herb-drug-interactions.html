Chinese Herbs and Your Medications: What to Tell Your Practitioner

By the Wongu Health Center team · October 9, 2026 · 5 min read

“It's natural, so it's safe” is one of the most common things we hear about herbs, and it isn't quite right. Herbs are used in Chinese medicine precisely because they have real effects on the body. That also means some of them can change how prescription medicines work. The good news: most problems are avoidable if your practitioner knows everything you take.

How Herbs and Medicines Interact

Interactions usually happen in one of two ways:

  • The herb changes how much medicine reaches your blood. Some herbs speed up or slow down the liver enzymes that break medicines down, so a drug can end up too strong or too weak.
  • The herb and the medicine do the same thing. For example, an herb that thins the blood taken with a blood-thinning medicine can add up to a bigger effect than either one alone.

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) points out that many reported interactions come from lab or animal studies, and good clinical studies in people are limited.[1] That uncertainty is exactly why we take a careful approach rather than assuming an herb is fine.

Medicines That Need Extra Care

Blood thinners (warfarin, apixaban, clopidogrel, daily aspirin)

This is the most important category. Reviews of herb–warfarin interactions have found that Dang Gui (Angelica root) and Dan Shen (Salvia root) are among the herbs with the strongest evidence of increasing warfarin's effect and bleeding risk.[2] Ginkgo taken with warfarin has also been linked to a higher risk of major bleeding.[1] If you take any blood thinner, your practitioner will avoid or carefully adjust herbs that affect circulation, and may coordinate with your doctor.

Blood pressure and heart medicines

Gan Cao (licorice root) appears in many Chinese formulas in small amounts to balance the other herbs. In larger amounts or over long periods, licorice can raise blood pressure and lower potassium.[3] That matters if you take blood pressure medicine, diuretics (“water pills”), or digoxin.

Diabetes medicines

Some herbs, including ginseng, may affect blood sugar. Combined with insulin or other diabetes medicine, that could push blood sugar lower than expected. If you have diabetes, keep checking your levels as usual when starting a formula and tell us about any lows.

Antidepressants, transplant drugs, HIV medicines, and birth control

These are well known for interacting with supplements. St. John's wort, a popular Western herb rather than a Chinese one, is the best-known example: NCCIH notes it can weaken many medicines, including birth control pills.[1] If you take any of these, tell us about every supplement you use, not only what we prescribe.

What to Bring to Your Appointment

The easiest way to stay safe is to bring a complete list, or simply bring the bottles. Include:

  • Every prescription medicine, with the dose
  • Over-the-counter medicines you take regularly (pain relievers, allergy pills, antacids)
  • Vitamins, supplements, protein powders, and “wellness” teas
  • Any herbs or herbal products from other practitioners or stores
  • Upcoming surgery or dental work. Many herbs should be stopped beforehand, so let us know the date.
  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding, or trying to conceive

It works both ways: tell your doctor and pharmacist about your herbal formula too. They can check it against your prescriptions, and they need to know about it before any procedure.

How Our Practitioners Approach Herbal Safety

At Wongu Health Center, herbal formulas are chosen by our practitioners after reviewing your health history, current medications, symptoms, tongue, and pulse. Student interns' herbal recommendations are reviewed by a licensed Oriental Medicine Doctor. If an herb isn't a good fit with your medications, there is usually another option, or we may suggest acupuncture alone.

Tell your practitioner about all medications and supplements you take. Herbal formulas may interact with medications and may not be appropriate during pregnancy or for certain medical conditions.

Never stop or change a prescription medicine on your own to make room for herbs. If you have unusual bruising or bleeding, dizziness, swelling, or a rash after starting a formula, stop the herbs and contact your doctor and our clinic.
